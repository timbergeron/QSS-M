GPU timing · what remains expensive

Sampled asynchronous GPU timestamps now distinguish the render interval from CPU time inside frame presentation. On this RTX 4070, the windowed 800 × 600 GPU interval is approximately 27 µs while the CPU swap interval is 230 µs. At 4K the GPU interval rises to 190 µs. Driver/presentation work remains the largest CPU stage at low resolution; at 4K, rendering also matters. The exact driver/Windows cause has not been isolated.

Why your live QSS-M FPS can be higher

The chart measures first timedemos in fresh processes, not warmed live gameplay. Its discarded warm-up runs in a separate process. It warms OS/driver caches, but cannot preserve engine state for the measured passes. On the same PC and identical QSS-M executable, Aerowalk rose from 2,102 FPS on the first pass to 2,840 FPS at the repeat-pass median in one process.

The benchmark's native settings query confirms r_shadows 0 and r_outline 0, with scene caching on its automatic default. The first-pass penalty also appears with scene caching disabled, so scene-cache construction alone does not explain it. Demo reloads reset scene caches. These checks demonstrate a warm-process effect; they do not identify a specific driver, shader, or CPU-cache cost.

Presentation is the strongest measured lead

A private source build measured ordinary demo playback, skipped 3,000 rendered frames, then aggregated five seconds. At 800 × 600 windowed, approximately 198 µs per frame elapsed inside SDL_GL_SwapWindow, compared with 85 µs in rendering/HUD submission. In the fullscreen probe the swap interval fell to 125 µs. That interval includes driver work and potential waits for the GPU; GPU timestamps are needed to separate rendering from presentation overhead. The sample covers part of the turn and is not comparable to a full timedemo score.

Optimization priorities

  1. Separate GPU rendering from present waits. Add asynchronous GPU timestamps to the isolated profiler, compare matched windowed/fullscreen runs at 800 × 600 and 4K, then optimize the measured source of waiting or GPU work. Preserve image quality and verify frame-time distributions.
  2. Remove repeated HUD resource lookups. The CPU stack sample finds SCR_ShowObsFrags searching for optional gfx/ibar2.lmp every rendered frame. The file lookup path accounts for about 5% of sampled time during normal playback. Cache the loaded or missing result and invalidate it through SCR_LoadPics on HUD/game reload. A short scr_obsitems 0 timedemo probe did not show a clear gain, so this is confirmed redundant work, not a proven explanation for the throughput gap.
  3. Measure demo processing separately. Timedemo accelerates packet parsing and rewind-state tracking; normal playback receives far fewer recorded messages per rendered frame. Optimize measured decoder/snapshot costs while preserving normal rewind behavior, and validate live gameplay alongside timedemo.
  4. Recheck all nine maps and all engines. Use the same in-process warm-up, matched rendering features, 800 × 600 plus 4K, and rotating engine order. Retain the existing launch/first-use results as a separate metric. Leading one view does not establish a lead across maps or workloads.

The optimized MSVC build also did not show a clear compiler-only improvement over the installed executable. The remaining gap has not been traced to one definitive engine defect. Those first diagnostic runs preceded the observer HUD cache change reported above; they did not replace the installed binary.

FPS method, settings, and limitations

Identical protocol-15 NetQuake demos recorded in QSS-M: a stationary camera turning approximately 360° over 20 seconds at the first deathmatch spawn on each map. Stock id1 game code, no monsters or active players. CTF maps test their geometry and stock entities; CTF gameplay is absent. This view samples one spawn and its visible surfaces, not the whole map.

One engine runs at a time. Every map/engine combination receives one discarded warm-up and five native timedemo measurements, with engine order rotated between passes. The run resumed after configuration checks; replacement ezQuake samples for some early maps were collected separately. Diagnostic samples are excluded. Each pass starts a fresh process; startup and map loading are excluded by the engine's timedemo timer. OS file and driver shader caches stay warm. The runner stops its owned process after the result has been written. Background throttling is disabled through native engine controls; Quakespasm-family clients use -listen -noudp to bypass their focus sleep without UDP networking. No active server or match is running.

800 × 600 physical client area, checked directly for all six engines; FOV 90, view size 100, VSync off, MSAA off, and uncapped requested FPS. Sound enabled. Same pak0/pak1 and BSP bytes; no personal .lit, .ent, replacement textures, or model packs. vkQuake uses Vulkan; the other engines use OpenGL. Renderer defaults, interpolation, particles, audio sampling/buffers, and internal timing paths differ, so image quality and workload are not perfectly identical. Native timedemo frame counts and timing precision are retained in the samples.

“ms / frame” is 1000 / FPS, an average-throughput equivalent. These tests do not measure 1% lows, stutter, input latency, or network play. Five passes show repeatability, not statistical significance. Cross-map summaries use the geometric mean of each map's median FPS, weighting all nine maps equally. The original lifecycle section compares FTEQW and QSS-M; the four additional engines are included in FPS.
